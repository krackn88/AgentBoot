NOTES     Clinical Practice Guidelines
          Clinical practice guidelines include recommendations intended to optimize patient care. Informed
          by systematic review of the evidence and an assessment of the risks versus benefits.
          CPG:
          • Not fixed guidelines
          • Establishes diagnostic and treatment modalities
          • Systematically developed statements to help guide clinical decision making
          • Identify which components of the decision-making process are objective.
          • Incorporate traditional “standards” of care with scientific research.


          Guideline development
           1. Gather best available research evidence through a detailed literature search about the
              appropriateness and effectiveness of different clinical strategies.
           2. The research evidence is used for guideline construction by small groups who represent as
              many interested parties as possible.
           3. The guideline is then tested by professionals in select settings who were not involved in the
              guideline development to review it for clarity, internal consistency, and acceptability.
           4. The guideline should be reviewed at specified periods of time and modified when new
              knowledge is gained.


          Trustworthy Clinical Guidelines Should Be
          Valid: should lead to predictable health gains and costs
          Cost-effective: improvements in health care should have acceptable costs.
          Reproducible: given the same evidence, another guideline development group would produce
          similar recommendations
          Reliability: given the same clinical circumstances; another health professional would apply the
          recommendations in a similar fashion
          Representative: from all key disciplines and interests, including patients
          Clinically applicable: to the target population in line with scientific evidence


          Clinical Guidelines That Are Trustworthy Should Be
          Flexible: by identifying exceptions, alternatives, and patient preferences in the decision-making
          process
          Clear: using precise definitions and user-friendly formats
          Meticulously documented: development process to detail who took part, methods used, and
          assumptions made, and should link recommendations to the available evidence, which should be
          graded according to its method (e.g., from randomized controlled trials [grade I] to uncontrolled
          or consensus studies [grade III])
          Reviewed periodically: and modified to incorporate new knowledge


          Established Clinical Practice Guidelines Should Be
          • Disseminated to potential users
            $ Publication in professional journals, sending guidelines to targeted individuals, as well as
              strategies involving an educational intervention
          • Implementation means ensuring that users subsequently act upon the recommendations
            $ Strategies to get users to adopt and apply guidelines
            $ Must address:
              • Barriers to behavioral change such as time, cost, staff, and other resources

Standards of Medical Care
Defined: level of professional medical care that is relevant for a specific medical situation
Liability: determines if a provider can be held liable for a patient’s injury
Acceptable: would another medical professional with similar credentials and experience and
access to the same resources have performed in the same manner?
Breach: a breach in the standard of care would require the plaintiff to establish that:
• The provider owed a duty of care to the patient
• The provider breached that standard of medical care
• The plaintiff suffered harm or injury
• That there was a causal connection between the provider’s negligence caused or contributed to
   the harm or sustained injury


Examples of Breaches of the Standard of Care
Failure to diagnose: failing to diagnose a disease
Misdiagnosis: diagnoses the wrong condition
Lack of follow-up care: Follow-up care is a crucial part of the process (Standards of Practice).
Misreading or misinterpreting lab results: Diligent practice in reading and interpreting results upon
which to make decisions.
Prescription drug errors: prescribing the wrong drug or the wrong dose or for the wrong condition
(off-label)
Intentional harm intentionally inflicting harm on a patient easily qualifies as a breach of the
standard of care. Criminal charges may also result


Risk of Standard of Care Breach
Occurs either by:
• Error
• Omission
• Delay
• Inappropriate use of available resources

          Five Steps of the Evidence-Based Process
          1. Ask a clinical question.
             Research question structured using PICO-T
             (Patient/population, intervention, comparison, outcome, time)
          2. Obtain the best research literature.
             Steps include: literature search, literature search databases,
             key words/search tips, article selection, critically appraise the
             evidence, grade the evidence.
          3. Critically appraise the evidence.
             Different criteria can be used to make determinations, but
             typically include study design, quality of the evidence, and consistency
          4. Integrate the evidence.
             Evidence-based decision making includes integrating the best scientific evidence with clinical
             expertise and patient preferences and values
          5. Evaluate the outcomes.
             Monitoring and analyzing the results of evidence-based decision making

NOTES     Qualitative Designs Defined
          Phenomenologic studies examine human experiences through the vivid descriptions or
          interpretations that are provided by the people involved.
          Ethnographic studies collect data from groups, such as certain cultural groups in their natural
          environment.
          Grounded theory studies Constant comparison process is used to compare data that have already
          been gathered. Pertinent concepts are identified, analyzed, and theories developed. (e.g., to
          understand key reasons that people do not keep follow-up appointments)
          Historical studies Historical data is identified, located, evaluated, and synthesized. The data is
          subjected to both external (authenticity) and internal (accuracy) criticism (e.g., Pearl Harbor)

Patient Informed Consent
Informed consent is essential to patient autonomy.
Informed consent may be for:
   Medical treatment or intervention
   Research purposes
Informed consent requires discussion and documentation of:
 1. the nature of the procedure,
 2. the risks and benefits and the procedure,
 3. reasonable alternatives,
 4. risks and benefits of alternatives, and
 5. assessment of the patient’s understanding of elements 1 through 4.


Patient Informed Consent
Additionally:
1. The provider is obligated to clearly document the patient’s participation in the decision-
   making process.
2. Avoid any suggestion of coercion or manipulation of the patient to agree.
3. The provider must make a recommendation and provide their reasoning for said
   recommendation.


Three Legal Approaches to Ensure Adequate Informed Consent
1. Subjective standard: What would this patient need to know and understand to make an
    informed decision?
2. Reasonable patient standard: What would the average patient need to know to be an
    informed participant in the decision?
3. Reasonable provider standard: What would a typical provider say about this procedure?
Reasonable patient standard is the most commonly used approach because it focuses on what the
average patient needs to know.
The provider is responsible for determining the approach given the situation at hand.

Assimilation
Occurs when two cultures meet, and one culture completely absorbs the other culture, with the
people adapting fully till the point that the host culture is unrecognizable from the native culture.
Acculturation
Occurs when two cultures meet, and the group adapts a few of the native culture’s practices;
however, there are still identifiable differences between the native culture and the host culture.

Open questions draw out and explore the person’s experiences, perspectives, and ideas. Invites

necessarily agreement.

Four Fundamental Processes of MI
1. Engaging: Establish a strong, productive working relationship.
2. Focusing: Establish a direction (agenda) that is mutually agreed-on. Gives the clinician
   permission to move into a directional conversation about change.
3. Evoking: Gentle exploration of the motivation for change. Guides the person to build their
   own “why” of change through eliciting the patient’s ideas and motivations.
4. Planning: Develop a change plan that is acceptable, accessible, and appropriate.
