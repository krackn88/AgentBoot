          Role of the Primary Care Provider
          • Differentiate normal aging from pathologic aging
          • Identify the patient’s goals for health
          • Provide recommendations for health promotion
          • Prevent injury, illness, and harm
          • Maintain function and engagement in community


          Effects of Aging on the Body
          • Aging affects the major organs in several
            ways:
            $ Less tissue mass
            $ Fewer functional cells
            $ Reduced compliance
            $ Diminished perfusion
            $ Poorly coordinated systemic responses
          • These changes predispose the body to
            disease!


          Normal Aging
          Normal aging follows a predictable pattern,
          but the rate of aging and extent of decline
          vary.


          Physiologic Aging
          • Pace and extent of aging depend on
            genetics, lifestyle, and environment.
          • Expected changes
            $ Natural rhythms of the body change—affects sleep, stress, digestion
            $ Less dynamic variability—HR, temp
            $ Reduced physiologic reserve

Pathologic Aging                                                                                          NOTES
• Aging causes physiologic changes that create a predisposition to disease.
  $ E.g., decreased gut motility & intestinal flora ➔ anorexia and nutritional deficiency ➔
    weakens the intestinal walls ➔ diverticulosis, constipation, bowel obstruction
• Disease is more debilitating than in younger adults.
• Drugs used to treat diseases may work differently in older adults, which can result in harm.

• Consider goals of care to be part of your assessment, especially in older adults.
  $ What knowledge does the patient have about their health?
  $ What are the patient’s goals and values?

Goals of Care
• Often, the goal is to alleviate suffering.
  $ Suffering can be experienced by patient or loved ones.
  $ Suffering can be caused by disease or treatment.
  $ Suffering is individualized.
    • Pain, fear, debilitation, death
    • Financial burden
    • Loss of time

          Falls
          • 3 million older adults are treated in the emergency
            department (ED) for falls each year.
          • About 20% of falls result in a serious injury, such as fracture or
            traumatic brain injury.




          Fall Risk Assessment
          • Three-question screening
            $ Do you feel unsteady when standing or walking?
            $ Do you worry about falling?
            $ Have you fallen in the past year?
               • If yes, how many times?
               • If yes, were you injured?
          • If yes to any question, the patient is at increased
            risk for falls.


          Fall Risk Assessment
          • At-risk patient
            $ Assess the patient’s gait, strength, and balance.
              • Several available tests: Timed Up & Go, 30-Second Chair Stand, or 4-stage Balance Test
            $ Perform medication review.
            $ Discuss home hazards.
            $ Perform orthostatic blood pressure assessment.
            $ Check the patient’s visual acuity.

                              GERIATRIC SYNDROMES

Frailty
• Geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiologic,
  psychologic, and/or social function
• Often occurs in patients with multiple comorbidities and
  stressors
• Increases the risk of morbidity (hospitalization, need for
  residential care, loss of mobility) and mortality

Assessing Frailty                                                               © South_agency/iStock
Physical Frailty Phenotype (Fried scale)
• Unintentional weight loss greater than 10 pounds per year (or more than 5% body weight)
• Self-reported exhaustion after exertion or activity
• Weakness in grip strength
• Slow walking speed (more than 6 or 7 seconds to walk 15 feet)
• Reduced energy expenditure from low physical activity
Score of 3 or more indicates frailty.

Interventions for Frailty
• Nutrition
  $ Low-volume, high-calorie snacks
• Fall prevention
• Exercise
  $ Mobility, gait, and muscle strengthening to improve
    performance, well-being, and bone density
• Medication management/deprescribing
• Referral:
  $ For Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment
  $ Home health
  $ PT/OT


Sleep Disturbances
Insomnia: problem with initiating, maintaining, or
consolidating sleep or experiencing poor sleep quality
• Must impair cognition/mood/function/energy
• Not due the environment
• Not explained by another disorder (i.e., sleep apnea)

Managing Sleep Disturbances
• Sleep hygiene for everyone!
  $ Regular schedule for sleep, exercise, and meals
  $ Avoid stimulants, evening fluids, and alcohol.
  $ Comfortable sleep environment: sleep only!
  $ Avoid napping.
  $ Get up if awake after 20 minutes.
• First-line treatment in older adults
  $ Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)

Renal Insufficiency                                                                                    NOTES
• Pathologic reduction in kidney function
  $ Reduced eGFR: <90 mL/min/1.73 m2
  $ Elevated BUN/creatinine, proteinuria may be present
  $ Radiology, abnormal pathology may be present
  $ Urine output may be decreased


Renal Insufficiency: Causes
• Uncontrolled chronic diseases                    • Acute conditions
  $ Diabetes mellitus                                $ Dehydration
  $ Hypertension                                     $ Infection
  $ Cardiovascular disease (CVD)                     $ Medication toxicity
  $ Rheumatologic diseases                           $ Ischemia
  $ Others                                         • Medication use
                                                     $ Nephrotoxins


Renal Insufficiency: Treatment
• Assume it is acute until proven otherwise!
  $ Remove offending medications.
    • Nephrotoxins
    • Medications that depend on renal clearance
    • Medications that reduce renal blood flow
  $ Hydrate the patient.
  $ Check for infections.
  $ Control chronic diseases.


Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
• Estimated GFR (eGFR)
  $ <60 mL/min/1.73 m2
• Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR)
  $ >30 mg/g of urine
• Changes present for more than 3 months
• Remember: Creatinine is a by-product of muscle metabolism.
  $ Muscle mass decreases during normal aging process.
  $ Can alter interpretation of creatinine and overall renal function

Orthostatic Hypotension
• Significant drop in blood pressure (BP) that occurs upon standing
  $ Systolic BP drops by 20 mmHg or more.
  $ Diastolic BP drops by 10 mmHg or more.
• Lightheadedness, syncope, and angina may occur when changing positions.

NOTES     Orthostatic Blood Pressure Measurement
          • Patient lies down for 5 minutes.
            $ Measure BP and pulse.
          • Patient stands for 1 minute.
            $ Measure BP and pulse.
          • Patient stands for an additional 2 minutes.
            $ Measure BP and pulse.
          Orthostatic hypotension = SBP decreases by >20 mm Hg, or DBP decreases >10 mm Hg

          Orthostatic Hypotension: Predisposing Factors
          • Medications                                      • Alcohol and large meals
            $ Antihypertensives                              • Medical disorders
            $ Vasodilators                                     $ Neurodegenerative disease
            $ Nitrates                                         $ Diabetic neuropathy
            $ Tricyclic antidepressants                        $ Autonomic dysfunction
            $ Opiates


          Orthostatic Hypotension Management
          • Avoid supine position.
          • Avoid triggers.
            $ Alcohol, large meals, excessive heat, high-intensity exercise
          • Physical maneuvers to increase BP (increase cardiac output)
            $ Leg crossing, squatting
            $ Increase fluid intake
          • Medication review

          Pain in Older Adults
          • 40% of ambulatory older adults have chronic pain, usually in the hips, back, or knees.
          • Goals of pain management
            $ Reduce pain intensity.
            $ Improve function.


          Pain Management
          Step 1:
          Start with nonpharmacologic interventions. Continue to use these
          even if medications are added!
          Step 2:
          Try low-risk pharmacologic agents (acetaminophen) or topical
          NSAIDs.
          Step 3:
          Try oral NSAIDs if no contraindications.
          Pain Management
          Step 4:
          Aspirin or tramadol
          Step 5:
          Opiates (risk–benefit analysis)

          Competence
          • Legal term for global decision-making ability
          • Mental competence is presumed until proven otherwise.
            $ Not automatically determined by diagnosis or status
            $ Task-specific
            $ Not determined by outcome
               • Competent people make bad decisions all the time!
          • State laws vary.


          Capacity
          • Medical term for the ability to make decisions about care and treatment
          • Four components
            1. Understanding
            2. Appreciation
            3. Reasoning
            4. Communication


          Provider Role in Assessing Capacity
          • Assess for communication barriers.
          • Assess for understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and communication.
          • Utilize formal capacity assessment tools.
            $ Aid to Capacity Evaluation
            $ Hopkins Competency Assessment Test
          • Perform cognitive testing if it will assist the decision.
