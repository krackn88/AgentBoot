Acute Bacterial Rhinosinusitis (ABRS)
• Secondary bacterial infection of the sinuses
  $ Persistent symptoms: ≥10 days without improvement
  $ Severe symptoms: at onset and lasting at least 3 days
    • Fever, purulent nasal discharge, ill-appearance
  $ Worsening symptoms: symptoms improve, then worsen again (“double-sickening”)



Bacterial Sinusitis
                     Pathogens
                      • Streptococcus pneumoniae
                        • Risk for complications: orbital cellulitis, meningitis
                      • Haemophilus influenzae
                      • Moraxella catarrhalis
                     Management
                      • Amoxicillin/amoxicillin/clavulanate × 5–7 days
                        • Recent antibiotic exposure:
                          • High-dose amoxicillin/clavulanate ER (2000/125 mg
                            BID)
                        • Penicillin allergy:
                          • Doxycycline
                          • Third-generation cephalosporin

                                                                                           © Refluo/Shutterstock



          Allergic Rhinitis Management

                     Intranasal glucocorticoids (fluticasone, mometasone, beclomethasone)
                      • Preferred monotherapy for persistent symptoms (nasal & conjunctival)

                     Intranasal antihistamines (azelastine, olopatadine)
                      • Intranasal administration is superior to oral antihistamines.

                     Second-generation oral antihistamines (fexofenadine, loratadine, cetirizine)
                      • Less sedating than first generation

                     Intranasal decongestants (oxymetazoline)
                      • Beneficial with severe congestion administered prior to INCS or INAH

                     Systemic corticosteroids: very severe or intractable symptoms

                              Oral decongestants and leukotriene receptor antagonists:
                                           Risks may outweigh benefits

NOTES     Epistaxis Characteristics
          • Bimodal: children and older adults
          • Etiology
             $ Idiopathic (most common)
             $ Epistaxis digitorum (nose picking) / trauma
             $ Foreign body (children, dementia)
             $ Alcohol, cocaine use
             $ Infection/allergies
             $ Bleeding disorder*
             $ Anticoagulation*
             $ High blood pressure*
          * Higher risk for posterior/severe nosebleed


          Epistaxis Management
          Refer if hemodynamic stability, severe
          (duration >30 min or over a 24-hour period)
          Nasal compression:
          • Pinch nose tightly for ≥5 minutes.
          Nasal packing
          Topical decongestant
          • Oxymetazoline
          Cautery
          • Visualization of bleeding
          • Silver nitrate stick

NOTES     Hearing Screening
          • United States Preventive Services Task Force
            (USPSTF) does not recommend universal screening
            in adults.
          • Whisper test is useful for screening if the patient is
            not aware of hearing loss.
          • Audiometry referral for any complaint of hearing loss
            $ Determines
               • Type:
                 $ Conductive
                 $ Sensorineural
                 $ Mixed
               • Severity

                       1. Hearing loss will impact employment opportunities if not corrected.

          Conductive Hearing Loss
          Problem with outer/middle ear (i.e.,
          cerumen buildup, FB, etc.)
          • Sound cannot transmit through canal/TM.
          • Often caused by obstruction
             $ Cerumen impaction
             $ Foreign body (including
               headphones)
             $ Otitis externa
             $ Otitis media with effusion
             $ Eustachian tube dysfunction
          • Chronic causes are less common.




                                                                                                  Joshua Lanclos/APEA

          Sensorineural Hearing Loss
          • Due to pathology of cochlea, CNVIII, or CNS
          • Presbycusis is the most common cause.
            $ Age-related hearing loss due to genetic factors and noise exposure
            $ Difficulty hearing mid-high frequency sounds, speech distortion, tinnitus (crickets, ringing, bells)
          • Other causes
            $ Acoustic lesions, trauma, ototoxic medications, acute/chronic noise exposure, hereditary
               disorders, Ménière’s disease

Auditory Emergency
Refer to ENT or ER for:
Hearing loss that is:
• Abrupt onset
• Unexplained
• Unilateral
• Sensorineural hearing loss


Hearing Loss: In Office Testing
Weber Test
• Conductive loss: louder in affected ear
• Sensorineural loss: louder in unaffected ear

Rinne Test
• Normal: air louder than bone conduction
• Abnormal: bone louder than air conduction
