Abdominal Pain: History                                                                                         NOTES
History is critical to understanding abdominal pain.
Important to ask a lot of questions:
• Where is the pain?
• When does the pain occur?
• Is the pain associated with other symptoms (nausea, vomiting, bowel movement)?
• Does anything relieve the pain?


Abdominal Pain Clues
The source of pain may be “intra” or “extra” abdominal.

Acute Cholecystitis
• Inflammation of the gallbladder
• Presentation
  $ RUQ pain: steady and severe
  $ “Colicky” sometimes epigastric or radiating to right shoulder
  $ High-fat meal causes pain
  $ Fever
  $ Movement makes the pain worse
• Commonly caused by gallstones
  $ Acalculous cholecystitis 5–10% cases

Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease                                                                            NOTES
Reflux can be physiologic or pathologic.




GERD: Diagnosis
Presumptive diagnosis
• Pathologic symptoms
• Symptoms occur ≥2 days per week
• Absence of alarm features

Treatment:
• Weight loss (if appropriate)
• Dietary Modifications
• First-Line: PPIs


GERD: Alarm Symptoms
• New onset age—60 years or older
• Evidence of GI bleeding (or new IDA)

                                                  REFER!
• Anorexia/weight loss
• Dysphagia/odynophagia
• Vomiting (obstruction)
• GI cancer in first-degree relative

Barrett’s Esophagus                                                                                       NOTES




Dysplasia of the esophagus that develops secondary to chronic GERD


Barrett’s Esophagus: Risk Factors
• Male sex
• Age 50 or older
• non-Hispanic White American
• Chronic GERD (5 to 10 years or more)
• Cigarette smoking
• Hiatal hernia
• History of Barrett’s/esophageal cancer in a first-degree relative

NOTES     Infectious Diarrhea
          • Viral: Norovirus, Rotavirus (most common)
            $ Self-limited
          • Bacterial: Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Clostridioides difficile, Staphylococcus, others
            $ Suspect if immunocompromised or severe illness lasting more than 1 week
          • Parasitic: Giardia, others
            $ Suspect if immunocompromised, international travel, or poor sanitation


          Diarrhea Red Flags
          • Rectal bleeding
          • Nocturnal bowel movements
          • Nocturnal or progressive abdominal pain
          • Weight loss
          • Anemia, elevated inflammatory markers, electrolyte disturbances
          Symptoms                                                 Differential Diagnosis
          Anal pain during defecation                              Anal fissure, rectal carcinoma

          Night sweats, fever, weight loss                         Malignancy, chronic infection or inflammation

          Diarrhea preceding or accompanying passage of blood      Colitis

          Change in frequency or caliber of stools                 Malignancy



          Inflammatory Bowel Disease
          Two types:
          • Ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease
          • Peak incidence: 15–30 years

          Presentation:
          • Diarrhea, bloody stools, abdominal pain, tenesmus
          • Fatigue, anemia, and sometimes weight loss

                                        TIP: Recognize and refer to gastroenterology.

Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

Viral Hepatitis
• ALT greater than AST
• Viral infection affecting the liver
  $ Five viral causative agents:
  $ A, B, C, D, E
• Hepatitis B, C, and D can cause chronic
  infections.




Hepatitis Screening Guidelines
Hepatitis Type         Screening Guidelines
Hepatitis A            • No specific recommendations
                       • Clinical decision-making based on risk: occupational, international travel,
                         homelessness, males who have sex with males, injection drug use

Hepatitis B            • One-time universal screening ≥18 years of age
                       • UNLESS serologic evidence of immunity
                       • Each pregnancy

Hepatitis C            • One-time universal screening ≥18 years of age
                       • Each pregnancy
                       • Annually for high-risk populations (e.g., IV drug use)
